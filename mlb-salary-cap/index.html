A proposal for MLB's next labor agreement

Let baseball grow
into its salary cap.

What if MLB could introduce a meaningful payroll floor now, protect high-spending teams from immediate cuts, and phase out the luxury tax automatically as the sport grows?

Discussion draft · Not an MLB or MLBPA proposal

The idea in one minute

Start with a $180M payroll floor, a $244M soft cap (luxury-tax threshold), and a $450M hard cap. Index the floor and hard cap to league-wide baseball revenue, with the hard cap rising more slowly than revenue. Raise the soft cap on a smooth, nonlinear curve that closes the luxury-tax band gradually and meets the hard cap when league revenue reaches $16B. At that point, the luxury tax disappears.

The $244M soft cap matches the 2026 CBT base threshold, rather than creating a new initial tax threshold. The $450M initial ceiling is designed to sit above the Dodgers’ roughly $433M estimated 2026 luxury-tax payroll, avoiding immediate forced reductions if comparable payroll definitions are used. The actual first-year ceiling must be checked against every team’s payroll when the agreement begins. All initial amounts are illustrative policy choices, not negotiated figures. The example assumes $12B of starting annual league revenue; actual audited baseline and payroll definitions would need agreement.

Keep MLB’s progressive luxury-tax tiers

Preserve the existing Competitive Balance Tax structure, including escalating rates for repeat payers and progressively higher surcharges for spending further above the soft cap. Unlike today’s fixed dollar offsets, the surcharge thresholds would move with the soft cap and compress as the luxury-tax band closes. No extra lines are needed on the main chart.

How the surcharge thresholds evolve

Let the remaining tax band be G(R) = H(R) − S(R). Set the three surcharge thresholds at S(R) + G(R) × 20/206, S(R) + G(R) × 40/206, and S(R) + G(R) × 60/206. This reproduces the current $20M, $40M, and $60M offsets at the illustrative starting point and makes every tier converge smoothly at $16B.

The tier compression is a proposed design choice, not an existing MLB rule. It preserves the initial tax schedule while eliminating all taxable payroll bands when the soft cap reaches the hard cap.

What the agreement must guarantee

  • A real player revenue share. Guarantee players at least 50% of a negotiated, independently audited definition of baseball-related revenue—not merely a payroll system calibrated around a 50% target. Any shortfall triggers automatic reconciliation payments.
  • Revenue that cannot be hidden. Include national and local media, tickets, sponsorship, licensing, and baseball-attributable concessions and parking. Apply robust related-party rules to stadium-adjacent businesses; do not automatically count unrelated real estate income.
  • A floor with teeth. Clubs below the floor must increase qualifying player compensation. Revenue-sharing transfers should not be a substitute for spending on players.
  • A genuine transition. The hard cap begins high enough to avoid forcing immediate cuts under the agreed payroll accounting rules. Existing contracts need explicit grandfathering or transition protection.
  • A tax that truly sunsets. Luxury tax applies only between the soft and hard caps. Once the thresholds meet at $16B, the tax ceases; the floor and hard cap continue to grow with revenue.

How the math works

Let R be audited annual league revenue in billions. The following example makes the floor and hard cap use different growth rates: the floor grows proportionally, the hard cap grows with the square root of revenue, and the soft cap follows a quadratic ease-out path that meets the hard cap smoothly at $16B.

Salary floor

\[F(R)=180\left(\frac{R}{12}\right)\]

Hard cap

\[H(R)=450\sqrt{\frac{R}{12}}\]

Soft cap / luxury-tax threshold

\[S(R)=\begin{cases}H(R)-206\left(\frac{16-R}{4}\right)^2,&12\le R<16\\[5pt]H(R),&R\ge16\end{cases}\]

Luxury-tax band

\[G(R)=H(R)-S(R)=\begin{cases}206\left(\frac{16-R}{4}\right)^2,&12\le R<16\\[5pt]0,&R\ge16\end{cases}\]

Definitions: R is audited league-wide annual baseball revenue in billions; F, S, H, and G are per-team payroll thresholds in millions. These illustrative equations apply from the $12B baseline onward. At $12B: F = $180M, S = $244M, H = $450M. At $16B: F = $240M, S = H ≈ $520M. The luxury-tax band then disappears.

Amounts are in millions of dollars per club. The $206M initial gap between caps shrinks quadratically to zero. Both the gap and its slope reach zero at $16B, so the tax sunsets without a kink in the soft-cap curve. The hard cap grows with exponent 0.5, slower than league revenue. This is one possible formula, not the only one.

At $16B in league revenue, the floor is $240M, and both caps are approximately $520M. Beyond that point, there is no luxury-tax band.

The real tradeoffs

This isn't a free lunch. A $450M hard cap could still constrain superstar contracts and high-spending clubs. A $180M floor could require substantial transfers to smaller-revenue teams. And a nominal 50% guarantee may not improve the players' position unless both sides agree on revenue and compensation definitions.

Because a hard cap growing slower than revenue could eventually conflict with a 50% player-compensation guarantee, the agreement must include an explicit reconciliation and adjustment rule. It also matters whether $450M is genuinely above every team's qualifying payroll at implementation. That needs verification using the precise agreed measure—not just public payroll estimates.

Still, this design offers a different bargaining frame: raise the bottom immediately, avoid an abrupt cut at the top, and make the transition depend on the sport's success.
